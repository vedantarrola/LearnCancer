Lessening the impact of cancer

Cancer treatment changes daily life for the whole family. These steps can make it easier on a child's body, feelings and future.

Keep routines

Regular bedtimes, meals, play and schoolwork, where possible, help a child feel safe and normal.

Stay connected to school

Hospital teachers, video calls with the class and school support plans help children keep up and keep their friends.

Treat side effects early

Give anti-nausea medicine on schedule, and tell the care team about pain, fever, sleep or mood changes right away.

Eat, move and rest

Small, frequent meals, gentle activity when the care team says it is okay, and plenty of rest help the body recover.

Care for feelings

Psychologists, social workers, child life specialists and support groups help with fear, sadness and stress.

Ease practical stress

Ask the hospital social worker about financial counseling, lodging, parking and help with school or work.

Plan for the long term

Keep a treatment summary and go to survivorship check-ups, which watch for late effects for years after treatment.

Ways to support someone with cancer

Family, friends, classmates and neighbors can all help a child and family through treatment.

Offer specific help

Instead of "let me know if you need anything", offer a meal, a ride, or picking up a brother or sister from school.

Keep in touch

Cards, messages, video calls and short visits remind the child and family they are not alone.

See the kid, not the cancer

Talk about favorite games, shows and friends. Children still want to be treated like children.

Protect from germs

Stay away when you are sick, wash your hands, and check before visiting, because treatment can weaken the body's shields.

Remember brothers and sisters

Siblings often feel left out or worried. A little time and attention just for them matters.

Support the caregivers

Parents need rest too. Offer to sit with the child, do the laundry, or simply listen.

Help the wider community

Donate blood or platelets, join a bone marrow donor registry if you are eligible, or support childhood cancer research.

Ask your care team

  • What is the exact type and subtype of cancer?
  • What is the stage or risk group, and what does it mean?
  • What is the treatment plan, how long will it last, and what is the goal?
  • Is there a clinical trial for this type?

Get ready

Get ready for your next visit

Knowing what to bring, what to ask and how to cope makes hospital days easier. Build a checklist for today, then use the tips below. The tips change with the age you pick at the top.

On the day

  • Arrive a little early. Check-in, parking and finding the right clinic often take longer than expected.
  • Check the eating and drinking rules. Some scans and procedures need an empty stomach. Ask the day before.
  • Write things down. Note answers, new medicines and the next appointment before you leave.
  • Know who to call. Save your care team's daytime and after-hours numbers in your phone.

About

Why LearnCancer exists

Teens with cancer face a long journey of unfamiliar procedures, medicines and hospital stays, and most of the education comes as long handouts or overwhelming conversations. The information exists. It just isn't built for teens.

LearnCancer is made for teens ages 13 to 17. It delivers bite-sized videos matched to your treatment stage, so you know what you will see, hear and feel before it happens, with straight facts and room for your own questions.

See how LearnCancer works

How it works

  1. Choose your language. Every page, video and checklist switches to English, Spanish or French. You can switch any time.
  2. Pick your step. Choose where you are in treatment, from diagnosis to going home.
  3. Watch and get ready. Watch a short video, learn the big words, and build a checklist for your next visit.

What we believe

Honest, but gentle

Teens cope better with straight, true answers than with surprises.

See, hear, feel

Each video explains what you will see, hear and feel, in that order.

Your voice matters

Teens deserve a say in their own care, alongside their parents.

Small bites

Short videos for one step at a time, so no one has to take in everything at once.

Where to use it

On your phone, a hospital-room TV, a tablet in the waiting room, or at home before the next visit. Watch with a parent or a friend, then talk about it afterwards.
